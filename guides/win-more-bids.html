Why you keep losing bids, and how to stop cutting price blind

Getting underbid hurts most when you don't know your own floor, so you drop the price on a guess and hope. Here's how to bid from facts instead of fear.

Losing a bid you wanted stings. The usual reaction is to assume you were too expensive and knock money off the next one. But cutting price without knowing your real floor is how a busy schedule still ends the year with nothing in the bank. Two things fix most of it: knowing the lowest number you can actually take, and following up on the quotes that go quiet.

1. Find your floor before you ever negotiate

Your floor is the lowest price that still covers your true cost plus the minimum margin you're willing to work for. If you don't know it, every price objection becomes a panic decision. If you do know it, you can hold the line, or walk, on purpose.

Work it backward from cost. Say a job's true cost (labor, materials, drive time, permit, a callback allowance, and your overhead share) is $1,200, and the least margin you'll accept is 15%. Your floor is:

floor = 1200 / (1 - 0.15) = 1200 / 0.85 = $1,412

Now you know: $1,412 is as low as you go. If the customer wants $1,300, that's below your floor and you can say no without second-guessing, because the number isn't a feeling, it's math. Most owners who "win every bid" are quoting under their floor and don't know it.

2. Winning the bid isn't only about price

Plenty of bids are lost on presentation and silence, not price. Two cheap fixes:

  • Look like the pro. A clear, itemized proposal that spells out scope and exclusions beats a number scrawled on a business card, even at a higher price. Customers pay for confidence.
  • Follow up. A quote that goes quiet isn't a no, it's usually a "not yet" or a "forgot." One short, specific follow-up a few days later wins back more jobs than any price cut. Most contractors never send it.
